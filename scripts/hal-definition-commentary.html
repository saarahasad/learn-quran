This is the opening of the chapter on the ḥāl (بَابُ الْحَال) — the author first gives Ibn Ājurrūm's short working definition, then the commentator unpacks it word by word, showing exactly what each term in the definition is doing and, crucially, what it's designed to exclude.

1Two meanings of "al-ḥāl" مَعْنَى الْحَالِ لُغَةً وَاصْطِلَاحًا

لُغَةًlinguistic sense
هَيْئَةُ الْإِنْسَانِ مِنْ خَيْرٍ أَوْ شَرٍّ — the state of a person, whether good or bad. A plain, everyday word for "condition."
اصْطِلَاحًاtechnical (grammarians') sense
الِاسْمُ الْفَضْلَةُ الْمَنْصُوبُ الْمُفَسِّرُ لِمَا انْبَهَمَ مِنَ الْهَيْئَاتِ — the "extra" noun, manṣūb, which explains what is ambiguous about a circumstance.

Ibn Ājurrūm's own wording is shorter — الْحَالُ: هُوَ الِاسْمُ الْمَنْصُوبُ الْمُفَسِّرُ لِمَا انْبَهَمَ مِنَ الْهَيْئَاتِ — and the commentator's version simply inserts the word الْفَضْلَة ("the extra/dispensable element") to sharpen it, as unpacked below.

2The definition, word by word تَحْلِيلُ التَّعْرِيفِ

Every phrase in the technical definition is there to rule something specific in or out. The commentary walks through each one:

1الِاسْمُ"the noun"
Covers two kinds of "noun-ness": (i) a genuine, explicit noun, and (ii) a verb that is re-interpreted (taʾwīl) as if it were a noun — because it functions the same way in the sentence.
جَاءَ مُحَمَّدٌ ضَاحِكًا
"Muḥammad came laughing" — here ضَاحِكًا is the genuine noun-form (i). Some grammarians analyse the same idea using the verb يَضْحَكُ ("he laughs") instead, which is only valid because يضحك is understood as paraphrasing/standing for ضاحكًا (ii) — the verb is re-read as functioning like the noun.
2الْفَضْلَةُ"the extra/dispensable element"
Means the ḥāl is not part of what's structurally required for the sentence to count as complete speech (الْكَلَام) by its own conditions. This one word is what excludes the خَبَر (predicate) from the definition — a khabar is not faḍlah, since a nominal sentence is not complete speech without it, while a sentence is already complete speech before the ḥāl is ever added.
3الْمَنْصُوبُ"the manṣūb [noun]"
Rules out anything marfūʿ or majrūr in its own case-ending. Only a noun carrying naṣb can be a ḥāl — this is why case matters so much in every example that follows.
4الْمُفَسِّرُ لِمَا انْبَهَمَ"which explains what is ambiguous"
Means the ḥāl clarifies something that was hidden or unclear about the traits/condition of the noun it describes — whether that noun refers to a rational, intelligent being (like a person) or not.
196 Ibn Hishām (al-Qaṭr, p. 329): the ḥāl is what is supplied after a sentence is already complete — the extra information it adds is not itself an independent clause carrying its own grammatical weight; it rides on top of an already-finished statement.

3What makes the ḥāl manṣūb عَامِلُ نَصْبِ الْحَالِ

A ḥāl is never manṣūb on its own — it is always governed (نُصِبَ بِـ) by the verb of the sentence, or by something that behaves like a verb even though it isn't one:

الْفِعْل the verb itself
مَا يُشْبِهُ الْفِعْل whatever resembles / functions like the verb

Four things fall under "resembles the verb":

Verb-like itemArabic termNote
the active-participle noun (fāʿil-pattern noun)اسْمُ الْفَاعِلe.g. ضَارِبٌ ("[one who is] hitting") — it carries the verb's meaning and can govern the same way
the infinitive / verbal nounالْمَصْدَرthe noun expressing the bare action itself
the adverb (ẓarf)الظَّرْفtime/place words functioning with implied verbal meaning
the demonstrative pronounاسْمُ الْإِشَارَةe.g. هٰذَا, ذٰلِكَ — carries a pointing/verbal sense ("I point to...")

4What the ḥāl can clarify مَا يُبَيِّنُهُ الْحَالُ

The commentary gives three sentences to show that a ḥāl can describe the subject alone, the object alone, or both at once:

جَاءَ عَبْدُ اللَّهِ رَاكِبًا "ʿAbdullāh came riding." — رَاكِبًا clarifies the state of the fāʿil (عبد الله) only.
رَكِبْتُ الْفَرَسَ عَلَجَمًا "I rode the horse, saddled/bridled." — عَلَجَمًا clarifies the state of the mafʿūl bihi (الفرس) only.
لَقِيتُ عَبْدَ اللَّهِ رَاكِبًا "I met ʿAbdullāh, riding." — رَاكِبًا is ambiguous by itself and can describe either the fāʿil (the speaker, "I met him while I was riding") or the mafʿūl bihi ("I met him while he was riding") — context decides.

5Beyond fāʿil and mafʿūl bihi مَجِيءُ الْحَالِ مِنْ غَيْرِهِمَا

The ḥāl is not limited to describing the subject or the direct object — it can also arise from the predicate, from a noun made majrūr by a preposition, or from a noun made majrūr inside a possessive compound:

1مِنَ الْخَبَرِfrom the predicate
أَنْتَ صَدِيقِي مُخْلِصًا
"You are my friend, sincerely/being sincere." — مُخْلِصًا is ḥāl describing the state bound up with the predicate صَدِيقِي.
2مِمَّا أُجْرِيَ بِحَرْفِ جَرٍّfrom a noun majrūr by a preposition
مَرَرْتُ بِهِنْدَ رَاكِبَةً
"I passed by Hind, [who was] riding." — رَاكِبَةً is ḥāl from هِنْدَ, even though هند itself is majrūr (by the preposition بِـ) rather than marfūʿ or manṣūb.
3مِمَّا أُجْرِيَ بِالْإِضَافَةِfrom a noun majrūr inside a possessive compound
أَنِ اتَّبِعْ مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ حَنِيفًا
Qurʾān, al-Naḥl 16:123 — "That you follow the religion of Ibrāhīm, ḥanīfan (upright/inclining to truth)."

iʿrāb of the Qurʾānic example

حَنِيفًا Ḥāl from إِبْرَاهِيمَ, manṣūb, sign of naṣb is the visible fatḥah.
إِبْرَاهِيمَ Majrūr — its sign of jarr is a fatḥah standing in for the kasrah (since it is a non-Arabic proper name, dīwān mamnūʿ min al-ṣarf). It is majrūr specifically because مِلَّةَ has been compounded (muḍāf) to it.
مِلَّةَ Mafʿūl bihi of اتَّبِعْ, manṣūb; simultaneously the muḍāf of the compound مِلَّةَ إِبْرَاهِيمَ.
197 al-Naḥl 16:123.
198 The commentator notes the author deliberately did not mention a ḥāl arising from the mubtadaʾ (nominal subject) itself, because the grammarians differ over whether that is valid — see Ḥāshiyat al-Ḥāmidī, p. 130.

In short: the ḥāl is a manṣūb, non-essential (faḍlah) noun that clears up an ambiguous circumstance — governed into naṣb by a verb or a verb-like word (fāʿil-noun, maṣdar, ẓarf, or ism ishārah). It most often describes the fāʿil or the mafʿūl bihi (or both at once, when the sentence is ambiguous), but it can equally arise from the khabar, from a noun majrūr by a preposition, or from a noun majrūr inside a possessive compound — as the Qurʾānic حَنِيفًا example shows.
