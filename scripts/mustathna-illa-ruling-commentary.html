Having established that إِلَّا is the core exception-particle, this section answers the single most practical question about it: what iʿrāb does the excepted noun (الْمُسْتَثْنَى) actually take? The answer isn't fixed — it depends entirely on the shape of the sentence before إِلَّا. The commentary sets out three distinct states.

1Three possible states أَحْوَالُ الِاسْمِ الْوَاقِعِ بَعْدَ إِلَّا

state 1 وُجُوبُ النَّصْبِ naṣb is obligatory
state 2 جَوَازُ الْبَدَلِ أَوِ النَّصْبِ either badal or naṣb is permitted
state 3 حَسَبَ الْعَامِلِ case follows whatever governs the sentence

Which of these three applies is decided entirely by the nature of the speech that comes before إِلَّا — specifically, whether it is complete or incomplete, and whether it is affirmative or negative.

2Four key terms defined تَعْرِيفُ التَّامِّ وَالنَّاقِصِ وَالْمُوجَبِ وَالْمَنْفِيِّ

Before the three states can be applied, the commentary defines exactly what each descriptive term means:

تَامٌّcomplete
أَنْ يُذْكَرَ فِيهِ الْمُسْتَثْنَى مِنْهُ — the noun that the exception is being taken from (مُسْتَثْنَى مِنْهُ) is explicitly stated in the sentence.
نَاقِصٌincomplete/deficient
أَلَّا يُذْكَرَ فِيهِ الْمُسْتَثْنَى مِنْهُ — the مستثنى منه is not mentioned at all; only إلا and the excepted noun remain.
مُوجَبٌaffirmative
أَلَّا يَسْبِقَهُ نَفْيٌ أَوْ شِبْهُهُ — not preceded by a negation or anything resembling one.
مَنْفِيٌّnegative
أَنْ يَسْبِقَهُ أَحَدُ هَذِهِ الْأَشْيَاءِ — preceded by an actual negation, or by "shibh al-nafy" (that which resembles negation).

Shibh al-nafy (شِبْهُ النَّفْيِ) — "that which resembles negation" — covers two more particle-types that behave like a negation for this purpose even though they aren't literally one: النَّهْيُ (the prohibition, e.g. لَا تَقُمْ) and الِاسْتِفْهَامُ (the interrogative, e.g. هَلْ قَامَ؟). Both count as "negative" contexts for the purposes of this chapter.

Combining these two axes (complete/incomplete × affirmative/negative) gives exactly the three real-world possibilities the commentary identifies — because an incomplete sentence is, by definition, always negative:

تَامٌّ مَنْفِيٌّYesYesState 2 — badal or naṣb

3State 1 — complete & affirmative تَامٌّ مُوجَبٌ

When the sentence before إلا is both complete (mentions the mustathnā minhu) and affirmative (no negation of any kind precedes it), the excepted noun has only one possible iʿrāb: manṣūb, obligatorily, as mustathnā.

قَامَ الْقَوْمُ إِلَّا زَيْدًا "The people stood, except Zayd."
قَامَ Māḍī verb, mabnī on a fatḥah.
الْقَوْمُ Fāʿil, marfūʿ — and this is the مُسْتَثْنَى مِنْه, which is why the sentence counts as تَامّ (complete).
إِلَّا Particle of exception, mabnī, no locus of iʿrāb.
زَيْدًا Mustathnā, obligatorily manṣūb, sign of naṣb is the visible fatḥah — no other reading is possible here.
خَرَجَ النَّاسُ إِلَّا عَمْرًا "The people went out, except ʿAmr." — same structure: النَّاسُ is the fāʿil and the mustathnā minhu (making the sentence تام), nothing negates the verb خرج (making it موجب), so عَمْرًا must be manṣūb.

In both examples the reasoning is identical: the mustathnā minhu is present (القوم / الناس) and no negation precedes the verb — so per the definitions above, the sentence is تَامّ مُوجَب, and State 1 applies without exception.

4State 2 — complete & negative تَامٌّ مَنْفِيٌّ

When the sentence is still complete (mustathnā minhu present) but now negative, the strict rule loosens: the grammarian is given a choice between two valid parsings.

مَا قَامَ الْقَوْمُ إِلَّا زَيْدٌ / إِلَّا زَيْدًا "The people did not stand, except Zayd." Both زَيْدٌ (marfūʿ) and زَيْدًا (manṣūb) are correct here — they simply reflect two different ways of analysing the same sentence.
زَيْدٌreading 1 — al-badl
Treated as بَدَلٌ (a substitute/replacement) for الْقَوْمُ — grammatically "following" it, and so taking the same case: marfūʿ, sign of rafʿ the visible ḍammah. The underlying sense is close to "none of the people stood — [I mean] Zayd [did]," with زيد replacing القوم as the true subject.
زَيْدًاreading 2 — al-istithnāʾ
Treated as an ordinary mustathnā governed into naṣb by إلا, exactly as in State 1 — sign of naṣb the visible fatḥah. The sense here keeps القوم as the subject and simply excepts Zayd from the negation.

Why does negation open up this second option? Because once the sentence is negated, إلا زيدٌ can be re-read as functioning like an appositional correction to الْقَوْم rather than a pure exception — a reading that isn't available in the affirmative State 1, where there's no negation for the substitution sense to attach to. Both parsings are grammatically sound Arabic; neither is "more correct" than the other.

5State 3 — incomplete, always negative نَاقِصٌ

When the mustathnā minhu is dropped from the sentence entirely — leaving only إلا and the excepted noun — the sentence is, by definition, نَاقِص (incomplete), and by definition also negative (a ناقص sentence is never affirmative). In this state the rule changes completely: the word after إلا is no longer governed by إلا at all. Instead it takes whatever case its own verb or governor in the sentence would normally assign it — exactly as if إلا were not there.

مَا Particle of negation (حَرْفُ نَفْيٍ), mabnī, no locus of iʿrāb.
قَامَ Māḍī verb, mabnī on a fatḥah.
إِلَّا Particle of exception, mabnī, "extra/cancelled out" for iʿrāb purposes — it does not govern زيد here.
زَيْدٌ Fāʿil of قَامَ, marfūʿ — because the mustathnā minhu was never stated, زيد itself steps into the empty subject slot that قام needs filled.

The working principle for State 3: mentally delete إلا and the negation together, and simply parse the rest of the sentence normally. Whatever role and case the remaining noun would take in that plain sentence — fāʿil, mafʿūl bihi, majrūr by a preposition, or anything else — is exactly the role and case it keeps once إلا is put back in. This is what "حَسَبَ الْعَوَامِلِ" (according to the governing factors) means in the author's original wording.

In short: the noun after إلا has three possible fates. If the sentence is complete and affirmative, it must be manṣūb (State 1). If the sentence is complete but negative, it may be either a badal matching the case of the mustathnā minhu, or manṣūb as mustathnā (State 2). If the sentence is incomplete — meaning the mustathnā minhu is never mentioned, which always implies negation — إلا is effectively invisible for iʿrāb purposes, and the noun simply takes whatever case its own verb or preposition would assign it (State 3).
